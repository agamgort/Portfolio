1. Relational Database Design for Multi-Attribute Soil Taxonomy Analysis

Database Design • SQL • Schema Mapping • Soil Taxonomy • Data Standardization
▾
The Objective: Bridge multi-disciplinary gaps in soil science by engineering a unified relational schema that normalizes complex attributes across engineering, agricultural, and geological data structures.

Methods & Functionality

Constructed a relational database architecture designed to enforce strict data constraints, physical locations, and spatial-temporal variables across complex geographic regions. The database implements comprehensive lookup tables and structural keys that enable precise querying of mechanical properties (drainage index, texture metrics) along with localized chemical indicators (pH levels, macronutrient variances).

Key Structural Information

  • Relational Architecture: Enforces strong database integrity rules requiring exact geographic coordinates and USDA taxonomy keys before a record can be registered.
  • Temporal Data Tracking: Features robust spatiotemporal logging designed to monitor structural adjustments, chemical degradation, and nutrient fluctuations within localized regions over historical project ranges.
  • Multi-Disciplinary Model: Unifies separate analytical variables—enabling an agricultural expert to evaluate chemical fertility profiles while an engineer queries identical spatial entries for stability factors and drainage behavior.

Key Conclusions & Takeaways

Standardized geological data frameworks remove structural discrepancies and inconsistent properties that often disrupt regional ground-truthing studies. This architecture demonstrates how structured data systems help reveal long-term soil changes, evaluate land stability for infrastructure, and optimize resource plans over time.

4. Spatiotemporal Floodplain Exposure & Vulnerability Analysis

Spatial Data Science • Spatial Autocorrelation (Getis-Ord Gi*) • OLS Regression • ArcGIS Pro
▾
The Objective: Quantify and map the clustering patterns of vulnerable elderly populations (age 65+) living inside Florida flood zones to optimize resource allocation for the Office of Floodplain Management (OFM).

Methods & Functionality

Spatial data processing methods utilizing census block group vectors from the FGDL and U.S. Census Bureau. The workflow runs Spatial Autocorrelation (Getis-Ord Gi*) using a K-Nearest Neighbor spatial weights matrix, followed by Multivariate Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Regression.

Key Structural Information

  • Spatial Clustering: Verified strong, non-random clustering with a Global Moran’s I of 0.103 and an extreme z-score of 317.81 (p < 0.0001), isolating High-High hotspots along the coasts and southern peninsula.
  • Regression Constraints: The initial model required removing an endogenous "percent tract in floodplain" parameter to prevent an artificial 96% R-squared loop. The refined model demonstrated that population density, poverty, and race are statistically significant factors (p < 0.001), while the low adjusted R-squared (0.0302) suggests coastal development and retirement migration patterns exert stronger influence on floodplain exposure.

Key Conclusions & Takeaways

Emergency preparedness planning cannot rely solely on traditional socioeconomic vulnerability indices. Elderly exposure hotspots strongly concentrate along coastal retirement corridors, requiring customized evacuation, infrastructure, and emergency management strategies for both high-density affluent developments and lower-income communities.

5. Mapping Lampposts for Infrastructure Optimization & Geospatial Field Mapping

Trimble GNSS Hardware • ArcGIS Field Maps • Proximity Buffers • Gap Analysis
▾
The Objective: Mitigate pedestrian safety gaps along the western corridor of George Mason University’s campus by evaluating existing streetlight infrastructure along Ox Road.

Methods & Functionality

Developed a field collection-to-analysis workflow using ArcGIS Online, ArcGIS Field Maps, and Trimble GNSS hardware. Asset coordinates and infrastructure attributes were collected in the field, processed within ArcGIS Pro, and analyzed through spatial proximity buffering techniques to identify illumination gaps and optimize future streetlight placement.

Key Structural Information

Field analysis revealed a major infrastructure imbalance between Braddock Road and Ox Road despite both corridors containing identical streetlight counts. Spatial buffering calculations established an optimized illumination threshold of approximately 170 feet, leading to the proposal of 17 additional streetlight locations to improve nighttime pedestrian safety conditions.

Key Conclusions & Takeaways

The project demonstrated how geospatial field mapping and proximity analysis can directly support infrastructure planning, public safety improvements, and evidence-based decision making for campus transportation corridors.

6. Crime Analysis & Socioeconomic Spatial Data Mining in Fairfax County

Python (Scikit-Learn) • PCA • K-Means Clustering • ArcGIS Pro • Spatial Analysis
▾
The Objective: Investigate how socioeconomic variables influence spatial crime distribution across Fairfax County, Virginia by integrating demographic datasets with statistical and clustering models.
Code available upon request

Methods & Functionality

Combined Fairfax County Police Open Data (2025–2026) with American Community Survey (ACS) 5-year demographic estimates using ArcGIS Pro and Python-based data mining workflows. Crime incidents were normalized per 1,000 residents to remove population bias before applying Principal Component Analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction and K-Means Clustering for census tract classification.

Key Structural Information

  • Dimensionality Reduction: PCA identified dominant socioeconomic gradients driving tract variation. PC1 (29.75% variance) represented a wealth-and-education axis where income and education correlated positively while poverty and unemployment loaded negatively.
  • Cluster Classification: K-Means clustering separated Fairfax County into six distinct socioeconomic-crime groups, distinguishing affluent low-risk tracts from structurally vulnerable areas with elevated crime prevalence.
  • Model Validation: Cluster performance was evaluated using Elbow Method optimization and Silhouette Scores, ensuring statistically distinct group separation and meaningful spatial interpretation.

Key Conclusions & Takeaways

The analysis demonstrated that crime distribution patterns are strongly associated with integrated socioeconomic conditions rather than raw population density alone. Findings support the use of spatial data science and clustering models to improve urban planning, public safety resource allocation, and identification of structurally vulnerable census tracts.

7. Determinants and Dynamics of Crime Intensity at Urban Transit Stops

Python • Poisson Regression • Spatial Computing • Pandas & GeoPandas • ArcGIS Pro
▾
The Objective: Investigate how localized sociodemographic factors and built environment indicators influence the count and specific type of crimes (assault, theft, and auto theft) occurring at bus stops across the Washington, D.C. metropolitan area.

Academic Context & Functionality

Co-Authors: Sydnee Baldwin, Amber Gamgort, Sara Von Hoene, Sarah Zak | Course: GGS 366: Spatial Computing (Dr. Armita Kar, GMU)

Engineered a spatial data pipeline merging tract-level American Community Survey (ACS) sociodemographic attributes and Open Data DC built environment layers directly onto disaggregated bus stop point geometries using a spatial within predicate in Python.

Methods & Structural Information

  • Predictive Modeling: Formulated three distinct, counting-based Poisson Regression models utilizing a 95% confidence interval ($p < 0.05$) to isolate statistically significant environmental and demographic risk vectors across assault, theft, and vehicle theft datasets.
  • Sociodemographic & Environmental Covariates: Stratified independent tract matrices by race, age, poverty rates, unemployment, sex, road connectivity density (grouped into highway, arterial/collector, and local classes), and spatial proximity to emergency infrastructure (police and fire stations).
  • Model Performance Insights: Model capacity varied drastically by crime type. The model for general theft demonstrated the highest explanatory strength ($\text{Pseudo-R}^2 \approx 0.378$), whereas the motor vehicle theft ($\text{Pseudo-R}^2 \approx 0.171$) and assault models ($\text{Pseudo-R}^2 \approx 0.125$) indicated that additional variables like foot traffic or localized illumination levels heavily drive violent crime variations.

Key Conclusions & Takeaways

Statistical outputs revealed that transit-related crime is profoundly dictated by localized movement, urban connectivity, and socioeconomic structures. High road density and proximity to emergency infrastructure acted as powerful positive predictors, mathematically establishing that crime concentrates in high-activity transit corridors. Conversely, higher educational attainment acted as a strong mitigation factor, revealing a significant negative coefficient ($-0.0246$) that links an increase in Bachelor's degrees per tract to a direct reduction in localized assault rates near public transit nodes.
